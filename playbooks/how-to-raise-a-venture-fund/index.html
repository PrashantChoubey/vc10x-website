How to Raise a Venture Fund

The seven stages of a first-time fund raise, from testing demand before you quit to the final close, with the techniques that worked for emerging managers and what the LPs on the other side are looking for.

The seven stages at a glance

Every fund raise is different, but the managers and allocators we interviewed describe the same sequence, and the same places where first-time managers lose months. Skipping a stage rarely saves time; it usually shows up later as a stalled raise.

The market you're raising into

Short answerHarder than a few years ago. LPs have more managers to choose from and fewer distributions to reinvest, so first-time managers have to be sharper about who they pitch and why.

Two forces shape a raise today. The first is supply. Matt Curtolo, a former institutional allocator who now advises LPs and GPs, says thousands of new firms have launched in recent years, so LPs are "spoiled for choice" and many pitches start to look the same. He cites one GP's count that of more than 900 Fund Is raised in 2021 and 2022, only about 200 had raised a successor fund by early 2026.

The second is liquidity. Nolan Bean, CIO of FEG Investment Advisors, says the pace of new commitments has slowed because long-time venture investors aren't getting money back.

None of this makes a raise impossible. It makes the order of operations matter more: the managers in this playbook who closed did most of the work before their first pitch.

Stage 1 of 7 · Before you quit

Is there a fund here?

Short answerFind out before you resign. Ask the people likely to back you for soft commitments, estimate how many will really convert, and put some of your own money into a few deals so LPs can see what the fund will own.

The riskiest moment in a first raise is the day after you leave your job with no idea who will commit. The emerging managers we spoke with reduced that risk months, sometimes years, in advance.

Technique · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Test your network before you resign

Kevin borrowed an exercise from his years as a financial adviser, where new hires had to prove how far their personal network could carry them. Before launching his fund, he:

  1. Listed everyone who might back him.
  2. Told them what the fund would do, how it would be structured and how much he was raising.
  3. Asked for soft commitments: if I do this, would you back me?
  4. Estimated the total, and what share would really convert at a first close.

Technique · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Warehouse a few deals with your own money

His second piece of advice: "take some of your own money and warehouse a few deals." The check size matters less than showing you can find companies, get in and explain why. Kevin later rolled those companies into his first close, which showed LPs he was all in and gave them a preview of what the fund would own.

Technique · Amber Illig, The Council

Build your first LPs before you need them

Amber ran an angel community of about 50 operators for three years before launching her fund. When she left her job, she had a group to go to straight away, and did her first close that same day. She then spent six to eight months building the portfolio, so later LPs "would actually be buying into a portfolio versus just a vision of a fund."

What it costs to start

Launching a firm takes cash before any fees arrive. Kevin says "250,000 is on the low end of what it takes," and found a small minority investor in his management company to cover working capital. Treat that figure as his experience rather than a rule; costs vary with structure and service providers.

Do this

  • Draw up a list of likely backers, with a soft-commitment amount and a realistic conversion rate for each.
  • Make two or three investments with your own money that look like the fund's portfolio.
  • Budget for at least a year of costs before management fees.

What LPs will check

  • Whether others are already committed.
  • Whether your early deals match the strategy you're pitching.
  • Whether you can survive a slow raise.

Stage 2 of 7 · Positioning

Why you, and not an established firm?

Short answerDon't pitch yourself as a smaller version of a famous firm. Position the fund around a specialization that's hard to copy, and around the one thing no competitor has: you.

LPs compare every new fund with the managers they already know. How you position yourself decides who you're compared with, and against a firm with decades of track record, you lose.

Technique · Diana Tenkova, Institutional Quality

Don't position against the giants

Diana spent 12 years organizing meetings between professional investors and fund managers before founding Institutional Quality, which advises emerging managers on fundraising. Her advice: your strategy "should be very difficult to replicate and essentially not easily accessible by established managers." You can aspire to become the next Sequoia, but "you cannot start as a direct competitor to these kinds of firms."

Technique · Matt Curtolo

Lead with the person, then the strategy

The most common mistake Matt sees is a depersonalized pitch that jumps straight into deals and portfolio construction. He'd rather start with what shaped you and why you're doing this.

Or, as he put it in a later episode: "as an LP, I'm buying you, not necessarily your fund."

Technique · Matt Curtolo

Show Fund IV in your Fund I deck

The managers Matt has seen build lasting firms showed, from Fund I or II, what they wanted Fund IV or V to look like. The vision rarely turns out exactly as drawn, but it tells an LP you're thinking about a 20-year relationship, and it keeps you from drifting into strategies you aren't skilled at.

Do this

  • Write your positioning in one sentence that an established firm couldn't say about itself.
  • Open your deck with why you're doing this, not where you went to school.
  • Add a page on what the firm looks like by Fund IV.

What LPs will check

  • Whether your background explains your strategy.
  • Whether the story is authentically yours or follows the market.
  • Whether the strategy stays consistent fund after fund.

LPs test this directly. See Test 1: Focus in How LPs Evaluate Venture Fund Managers.

Stage 3 of 7 · Fund size and structure

What's the right Fund I?

Short answerBig enough to prove the strategy, small enough to close. A realistic target builds momentum; an ambitious one that stalls costs credibility. Decide your GP commitment and team structure with your likely LPs in mind.

Fund size is the first number an LP reads, and it sends a signal about discipline. Every guest who'd been through a raise argued for being realistic.

Technique · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Right-size Fund I around the proof you need

Kevin first imagined a larger fund with a second partner. In a slow market, he set a floor instead: enough capital to prove the thesis, the founders he backs and how he adds value, and to build a platform he could scale for Fund II.

For his fund, that floor was about $10 million. Yours depends on your strategy and check sizes.

Rule of thumb · Diana Tenkova and Matt Curtolo

A smaller target builds momentum

"It's easier to build momentum with a smaller target fund size," Diana says, "because it's psychologically more realistic for investors to see you reach a close quickly." Matt's version: "be realistic about timeline, be realistic about size." You can still be ambitious, he adds, as long as you can explain why the number is what it is.

Technique · Kevin Moore and Lara Nuchowicz

Decide the GP commitment with your LPs in mind

Kevin funded his commitment from savings. Some LPs insist on cash in the fund from day one, he says, while others accept a commitment funded by deferring management fees, so it pays to know your LPs before you decide. Lara Nuchowicz of AR Capital, a single-family office, accepts fee-funded commitments from very young GPs who can't yet write a large check.

Model the options with the Fund Economics Calculator, which shows fees, carry and a cash or fee-waiver GP commitment.

Solo GP or partnership?

Matt describes the trade-off LPs weigh. A solo GP decides quickly and with conviction, but needs strong service providers for administration, tax and audit, and LPs worry about key-person risk. Yet "in a two-person partnership, if one of those people leaves," he says, that may be harder to resolve than a solo GP stepping away. Kevin, a solo GP, added a venture partner with operating experience and a group of outside experts who question every company before he invests.

Do this

  • Work out the smallest fund that proves your strategy, and why.
  • Ask likely LPs early whether they need your commitment in cash.
  • Line up fund administration, audit and legal before you pitch.

What LPs will check

  • Whether the fund size matches your check sizes and ownership targets.
  • How much the partners are committing, and in what form.
  • What happens if a partner leaves.

Stage 4 of 7 · Who to target

Which LPs will actually back a first fund?

Short answerBuild a profile of your ideal LP before you build a list. Match each LP's typical check size to your fund size, start with the LPs already inclined to back emerging managers, and meet the large institutions now for later funds.

The most common waste of time in a first raise is pitching LPs who could never invest. The fix is to qualify LPs before you meet them, not after.

Technique · Diana Tenkova, Institutional Quality

Build an LP profile before a list

Her checks:

  • Size fit: compare your fund size with the LP's usual check. For large institutions, the minimum ticket can be too big a share of a first fund.
  • Family offices: often more flexible on first funds, especially when you invest in the sector where the family made its money.
  • Funds of funds: check their own fundraising timing. Approaching them while they're raising may not work.

Technique · Matt Curtolo

Start with the LPs who already lean in

Asked how he'd raise a fund himself, Matt started with the ideal customer profile.

A $25 million Fund I is unlikely to get a check from a sovereign fund or an insurer, he says: large LPs have annual budgets and limits on how much of a fund they'll own. "The larger LPs are good to talk to now for future fundraises just so they can get to know you." Funds I and II have usually been backed by funds of funds, family offices and individuals, plus a few endowments with emerging-manager programs.

Framework · Nolan Bean, FEG Investment Advisors

Know each LP type's appetite for risk

Nolan advises endowments, foundations and healthcare systems. On average, he ranks risk appetite, and tolerance for locking money up for a decade, in this order:

  1. Family offices
  2. University endowments
  3. Other nonprofits, such as community and private foundations
  4. Pension plans and healthcare systems, the most conservative

Where guests disagree: family offices

Diana calls family offices a strong fit for first funds. Kevin found many now invest directly and wanted co-investment he couldn't yet offer. Amber was told by everyone that first funds are "all family offices," but she'd never met one, and raised mostly from individuals and other VC funds, which back emerging managers to share deal flow. Her conclusion: different GPs connect with different LPs. One successful GP she knows only raises from institutions; another only from family offices.

Do this

  • Write your ideal LP profile: type, check size, sector link, appetite for first funds.
  • Ask each LP for their minimum check early, and move on if it doesn't fit.
  • Keep a separate list of large LPs to meet for Fund II or III.

What LPs will check

  • Whether you understand their mandate before the meeting.
  • Whether their check would be too large a share of your fund.
  • Who else like them has committed.

Stage 5 of 7 · Outreach

How do you get the meeting?

Short answerWarm introductions first. When you reach out cold, lead with what you have in common rather than a pitch, and start conversations in batches so momentum builds on itself.

LPs see more pitches than they can take. The managers who get meetings make an introduction easy to accept, and make every conversation feel like part of something already moving.

Technique · Diana Tenkova, Institutional Quality

Lead with common ground, not a pitch

To find that common ground, she suggests:

  • Ask other GPs for introductions to their LPs, and existing LPs for introductions to others.
  • Research before you pay for databases. In some European countries, commercial registers list the investors in closed funds.
  • Start in your local market, where connections are usually stronger.

Technique · Jessie Jia Guo, Next Legacy Partners

Write the cold email LPs answer

Jessie, who replies to every email she receives, looks for three things in a cold note: conviction in what you do, homework on her firm's mandate, and an interest in a relationship and a dialogue before an ask for money.

Technique · Amber Illig, The Council

Start your conversations in a batch

Amber's biggest lesson came from watching how the founders in her portfolio raise. The best know who they want to raise from before they start, and kick off all those conversations at once.

Send your target list to everyone you know and ask who they can introduce you to, then mine your own connections for paths to the rest.

Technique · Matt Curtolo

Build advocates, and tell your story to many at once

LPs talk to each other. "If I see a great manager I'm going to tell my friends about it," Matt says. A few advocates in your network can open more doors than a database. He also recommends publishing: writing, a podcast or talks let LPs hear your story at length before you meet, and let you tell it once to many people.

Why cold inbound is hard

Ned Brines, Chief of Investment Strategy at Arnel & Affiliates, replies to every manager who emails, but after analyzing where his deals came from, his team found few came from cold email. He now batches inbound once a week and takes 30-minute calls on a single day. It's one more reason to work on warm paths first.

Do this

  • Send your target list to your network and ask for specific introductions.
  • Write first emails around common ground, not the fund.
  • Aim to start around 20 conversations within the same couple of weeks.

What LPs will check

  • Whether you've done homework on their mandate.
  • Who introduced you, and what they say about you.
  • Whether your public writing or talks match your pitch.

Stage 6 of 7 · Meetings and momentum

How do you keep LPs moving?

Short answerUse the first meeting to establish fit, not to sell. Agree a next step before every call ends, share information in stages rather than all at once, and expect trust to take 12 to 18 months.

Most raises don't fail in the first meeting. They stall after the second, when the LP has everything they need and no reason to act. The managers who close design each conversation to lead to the next.

Technique · Diana Tenkova, Institutional Quality

Go in as an equal

Don't spend the whole 30 minutes on slides; ask about the LP's preferences and concerns. "The objective of the first call is not to sell the fund, but to establish fit and secure a second meeting." Matt agrees: the managers who get second meetings "listen more than they talk."

Technique · Matt Curtolo

Don't play all your cards at once

After a good first meeting, many managers send the data room and everything else. Matt warns that this ends the momentum.

Instead, add context in the second meeting and deeper information in the third, so each conversation earns the next. He calls the place where many raises die "this mid-funnel sort of trap," where LPs have had two meetings and nothing prompts them to decide.

Technique · Diana Tenkova, Institutional Quality

Leave every call with a next step

  • Agree the next step before the call ends, such as a follow-up meeting or an introduction to another partner, so the follow-up stays in your hands.
  • Have materials ready before anyone asks: detailed track record, deal list, one-pager, references, investment memos. "Delays cause interest to drop."
  • Tell LPs it's fine to say no, so you don't spend months chasing.

Framework · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Know, like, trust, in that order

Kevin credits a placement agent he knows with the rule he follows:

They know you through events, writing and podcasts; like you through good conversations; and trust you as you send investment updates and show how you work. Unless it's a strong referral, he expects that to take 12 to 18 months, and has met Fund I prospects he expects to invest in Fund III. Looking back at his first ten LP calls, he'd "just get to the point faster": ask early what they invest in, whether they're deploying, and whether they've backed managers like him.

Technique · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Do the institutional homework unprompted

One LP that passed told Kevin that managers who claim to be serious rarely complete a due diligence questionnaire. He spent a month filling one in, sent it, and got the meeting. The LP didn't invest in Fund I, but he believes it positioned him for Fund II.

Don't chase

Following up is part of the job; chasing isn't. Slava Darkhaev, who allocates to venture for a single-family office, calls constantly pushing for feedback "a big red flag" that signals desperation. Matt's pet peeve is over-communication: sometimes the right move is to take no for an answer and keep the relationship.

Do this

  • Plan what each of your first three meetings with an LP will cover.
  • End every call with a dated next step.
  • Complete a standard due diligence questionnaire before you're asked.

What LPs will check

  • How quickly you send what they ask for.
  • Whether your updates between meetings show progress.
  • How you handle a no.

Stage 7 of 7 · Closing

How do you get to final close?

Short answerClose what you have and start investing. Use several closes to show progress, give early LPs a reason to commit, set a date and communicate it, and measure your weekly activity rather than dollars raised.

Momentum is what closes funds. Each close gives hesitant LPs evidence that others are in, and a deadline that's real.

Technique · Matt Curtolo and Diana Tenkova

Close what you have, then close again

Asked for one piece of outdated fundraising advice, Matt named the idea that you need a set percentage of your fund at first close.

Diana recommends multiple closes for emerging managers: a completed first close "demonstrates momentum and encourages hesitant investors to commit," and lets you start investing. She also suggests incentives for early commitments, such as reduced management fees or co-investment rights.

Lesson · Amber Illig, The Council

Expect the second-close slog

Amber raised her fund in three closes during the 2022–23 downturn, and each felt different. The first was exciting, with early LPs keen to be first in. The final close had LPs sprinting in before the gate shut: about 18 in the last two weeks. The middle was the hardest.

Raising from many individuals and smaller funds is more like selling to consumers than to enterprises, she adds: more checks, smaller ones, and each takes time. She closed at least one LP every week of 2023.

Technique · Amber Illig, The Council

Measure inputs, not outputs

"It's very hard to be like, by the end of March I want to raise this exact amount of money," Amber says. Set targets for the number of LPs you talk to and introductions you get, then set a close date, communicate it and stick to it. "A consistent process is everything." When you're talking to many LPs at once, you can say so: three have committed, and the close is on this date.

After the close: earn Fund II

Your next raise starts the day this one ends. Matt says LPs value clear quarterly reporting and consistent communication, especially getting ahead of bad news. Aoifinn Devitt of Moneta Group adds that the managers who keep their place with an allocator act like partners: "I've always got more out of managers when they have overdelivered in terms of their ability to help me do my job."

Do this

  • Hold a first close as soon as you can start investing.
  • Decide what, if anything, early LPs get for committing first.
  • Set weekly targets for conversations and introductions, and a public close date.

What LPs will check

  • Who has already committed, and how much.
  • Whether the close date is real.
  • What reporting they'll receive afterwards.

The fundraising checklist

Thirty-five steps drawn from this playbook, grouped by stage. Work through them before your first LP meeting and keep them open during the raise.

Stage 1: Before you quit

  1. List everyone who might back you, with a soft-commitment amount for each.
  2. Estimate what share will convert at a first close.
  3. Make two or three investments with your own money that match the strategy.
  4. Budget for at least a year of costs before management fees arrive.
  5. Decide how you'll fund the management company in the meantime.

Stage 2: Positioning

  1. Write your positioning in one sentence no established firm could say.
  2. Explain why your background leads to this strategy.
  3. Open your deck with your story, not your résumé.
  4. Show what the firm looks like by Fund IV.
  5. Cut any theme you can't show you knew before it was popular.

Stage 3: Fund size and structure

  1. Work out the smallest fund that proves the strategy, and why.
  2. Check the size against your check sizes, ownership targets and reserves.
  3. Decide your GP commitment, and whether it's cash or funded from fees.
  4. Engage fund administration, audit and legal counsel.
  5. Decide solo or partnership, and how you'll cover what you can't do yourself.

Stage 4: Who to target

  1. Write your ideal LP profile: type, check size, sector link, appetite for first funds.
  2. Build a target list that fits that profile.
  3. Find each LP's minimum check before the first meeting.
  4. Check funds of funds' own fundraising timing.
  5. Keep a separate list of large LPs to meet for later funds.

Stage 5: Outreach

  1. Send your target list to your network and ask for specific introductions.
  2. Ask other GPs and existing LPs who they can introduce you to.
  3. Write first emails around common ground, not the fund.
  4. Start around 20 conversations within the same couple of weeks.
  5. Publish something that lets LPs hear your story before you meet.

Stage 6: Meetings and momentum

  1. Use the first meeting to establish fit, and listen more than you talk.
  2. Plan what each of the first three meetings will cover.
  3. End every call with a dated next step.
  4. Have your track record, deal list, references and memos ready.
  5. Complete a standard due diligence questionnaire before you're asked.

Stage 7: Closing

  1. Hold a first close as soon as you can start investing.
  2. Decide what early LPs receive for committing first.
  3. Set weekly targets for conversations and introductions.
  4. Set a close date, communicate it and stick to it.
  5. Plan quarterly reporting before the final close.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to raise a first venture fund?
Longer than most first-time managers expect. Kevin Moore of Serac Ventures says building enough trust for an LP to commit typically takes 12 to 18 months unless the introduction is a strong referral, and recommends spending 6 to 12 months testing your network before you even launch.
Which LPs invest in first-time venture funds?
Mostly individuals, family offices and funds of funds, plus some endowments with emerging-manager programs, according to Matt Curtolo. Large institutions often can't write a check small enough for a first fund, so many emerging managers meet them early for future funds instead.
How big should a first venture fund be?
Big enough to prove the strategy and small enough to close. Diana Tenkova of Institutional Quality says a smaller target builds momentum because LPs can see a close coming. Kevin Moore says institutional investors told him his fund needed to be at least about $10 million to read as a real Fund I rather than a "fund zero."
Do you need a minimum first close?
Not anymore, says Matt Curtolo, who calls it outdated advice: close what you have, start investing and put points on the board. Diana Tenkova recommends multiple closes for emerging managers, because each completed close encourages hesitant LPs to commit.
What should happen in a first LP meeting?
Establish fit rather than sell. Diana Tenkova advises approaching it "with an equal mindset rather than a desperate one," asking about the LP's preferences, and aiming for a second meeting. Matt Curtolo says managers who get second meetings listen more than they talk.
