The five questions every family office answers before it writes a check

Family offices are now one of the largest and least understood sources of capital for venture funds. They don't publish mandates, most don't have a website, and no two are alike. Michael Wallach, head of alternative investments at Levy Family Partners, repeats the line everyone in the industry uses: "if you know one family office, you know one family office."

Underneath the variety, the CIOs, principals and advisors we interviewed describe the same five questions. How a family answers them decides whether it invests in venture at all, how, and with whom.

Stage 1 of 7 · Who are we?

What kind of family office are you talking to?

Short answerWork out the structure, the size and the generation before you pitch. A family running its investments out of the operating business, a multi-family office and a 500-person single-family office make decisions in completely different ways.

Ronald Diamond, founder of Diamond Wealth, has invested alongside more than 100 family offices for over 20 years. At one end, he says, are offices with institutional teams of dozens of investment professionals. At the other, and far more common, is the family that sold a business and made a relative the CIO. Ken Goldman ran the top end: as president of Hillspire, Eric Schmidt's family office, he oversaw more than 500 people, with finance, legal, IT, properties and aviation all in-house.

Framework · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Know the three structures

Wendy Craft, CEO of Elle Family Office, a single-family office in Atlanta, describes three ways families organize, each with a different trade-off between cost, privacy and expertise:

Many families combine them: a single-family office for what the family knows, and a multi-family office for asset classes it doesn't.

Lesson · Ronald Diamond, Diamond Wealth

Size decides whether a single-family office makes sense

Diamond says that "in order to have a single family office make economic sense, you need a bare-bones minimum of $250 million." In our Family Office Roundtable he put the bar higher, saying "you need about 500 million", and went further: "85 to 90% of the family offices that exist shouldn't exist." Below that line, splitting legal, tax and administration across many families is cheaper. Wendy's advice to most families asking whether they need an office of their own is to ask why first, and for many, to use a good multi-family office.

Trend · Wendy Craft and Ronald Diamond, Family Office Roundtable

Watch for the move to outsourced CIOs

Ahead of the wealth transfer, Wendy says several first-generation families she knows have handed their investments to outsourced CIO firms with 40 or 50 staff, while keeping the trusts, estates and foundation in-house. A multi-family office can do everything from bill pay to governance; an outsourced CIO only invests. For a fund manager, that means the decision may now sit with a professional allocator, not the family.

AI inside the family office

Smaller offices are replacing analysts with software. "At least four of the family offices I know down here no longer have an analyst," Wendy says; AI screens deals against the family's criteria in minutes rather than weeks. Diamond adds that "there will always be a human in the loop" to make the final call. If you're raising, assume your materials may be screened before a person reads them, and make your fit with the family's criteria obvious on the first page.

For fund managers

  • Find out which structure you're pitching, and who actually makes the decision.
  • Ask about the generation in charge before anything else.
  • If the family uses an outsourced CIO or multi-family office, pitch them, not the family.

For families

  • Ask why you want an office of your own before setting one up.
  • Check the cost against a multi-family office at your size.
  • Decide what stays in-house and what's outsourced.

Stage 2 of 7 · Where did the money come from?

How does the source of a family's wealth shape what it invests in?

Short answerFirst-generation families stay close to how they made their money. They invest directly in what they know and back fund managers in what they don't, so the best pitch fills a gap in the family's expertise.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Ask the generation question

Wendy says that if you're talking to the first generation, "they built it and they're going to kind of stick to where they built that wealth." A second generation with an MBA is far more open to diversifying, and younger family members lean toward technology. So her first question to any family is about generation and origin, not money.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Michael Wallach and Ronald Diamond

Pitch to the gap, not the affinity

"The core of every family office is what they made their wealth in," Wendy says in our Family Office Roundtable. Families keep that sector in-house, often as a large, deliberate position whatever an institutional adviser might say about concentration, and hire managers for everything else. Diamond puts the rule simply: "If you made your money in venture, invest in venture, outsource the rest." Michael Wallach describes the Levy family doing exactly that, backing funds in the areas where it lacks expertise to build what he calls a "knowledge ecosystem" of specialist managers it can call on.

So pitching to families from your own sector often fails: they believe they can do it better themselves. Wendy's advice to real estate funds: "don't look for the real estate families, go find the hedge fund families."

How much goes to venture

Diamond says venture is typically 5 to 10 percent of a family office portfolio, and he has seen up to 25 percent in families that made their money in technology. Vishal Verma of Edgewood Ventures, a single-family office that has been running for about 30 years, says about 30 percent of the family's capital is in private markets, and emerging managers are "about ten percent, maybe fifteen percent" of its venture portfolio.

For fund managers

  • Research how each family made its money before you reach out.
  • Target families whose expertise doesn't overlap with your strategy.
  • Offer what they can't do themselves: access, judgment, specialist knowledge.

For families

  • Write down where you have a real edge, and where you don't.
  • Invest directly only where you have the edge.
  • Back specialists for everything else.

Stage 3 of 7 · How much time and liquidity do we have?

How do family offices allocate around taxes, liquidity and time?

Short answerFamily offices pay taxes, answer to no fund clock, and fund their own lives. So they judge investments after tax, value long horizons, and still want cash back sooner than most venture and private equity funds now offer.

Technique · Ned Brines and Jean-Baptiste Wautier

Use the time horizon as an edge

Ned Brines, chief of investment strategy at Arnel & Affiliates, a multigenerational family office, says "the biggest advantage families have over other institutions is, I call it time horizon arbitrage." His youngest beneficiary is eight years old, so the family is investing for the next 80 or 90 years. Volatility becomes an opportunity, and with no committee process to clear, "we could do it in a few hours if we really had to."

Jean-Baptiste Wautier, who spent 20 years at BC Partners, the last decade as CIO, and now runs his own family office, says the biggest change is your relationship with time: "your time horizon is not preset." With institutional capital, a fund has to exit on a schedule. With family capital, he can exit early if the moment is right, or hold on if it isn't.

Warning · Wendy Craft, Family Office Roundtable

Families still need liquidity, and lockups are getting longer

A long horizon doesn't mean families can lock everything up. Wendy says private equity has drifted from a five-to-seven-year investment to eight years with four one-year extensions, and many families are now wary of it.

What they're looking for instead, she says, is private investments with some distributions, and "maybe a two or three-year lock up." Michael Wallach makes the same point from a family with no fund deadline: "we are not going out to raise another fund, but we absolutely do want liquidity."

Jean-Baptiste Wautier's rule is to keep the two apart: "I never want liquidity to put pressure on this capital that goes into private companies." Ned's family does it with a low-leverage real estate portfolio that throws off steady cash, which frees the rest of the portfolio to take more risk. For the very wealthy, Salvatore Buscemi of Brahmin Partners says, the problem is often the reverse: "for the wealthy liquidity is a burden," because cash keeps arriving and needs somewhere to go.

Technique · Anurag Chandra and Ronald Diamond

Judge every investment after tax

Anurag Chandra is CIO of a single-family office and chairs the investment committee of a city pension system, so he sees both sides. Pensions don't pay tax; family offices do. His family office runs a barbell: liquid strategies with heavy tax optimization on one side, and an internal venture studio building new companies on the other.

Diamond says families are increasingly focused on "after tax returns than pre-tax returns because at the end of the day that's the only number that really matters." His example: private credit's 10 to 12 percent becomes 6 or 7 percent for a family in New York or California. Structure matters too. Wendy turns down deals that create a tax burden out of proportion to the investment: "If I have to file a tax return in 12 states to be in your little deal, I'm not going to do it."

For fund managers

  • Say how and when the fund expects to return cash, and offer a path to earlier distributions if you can.
  • Show what your structure means for a taxable investor: K-1s, state filings, timing.
  • Don't present pre-tax returns as the whole story.

For families

  • Hold enough liquid or income-producing assets that private holdings are never forced sales.
  • Compare investments after tax, not before.
  • Read the fund's term and extension provisions before committing.

Stage 4 of 7 · Do we build or buy?

Should family offices invest in startups directly or through venture funds?

Short answerThrough funds, unless venture is how the family made its money or it has a full-time team. Many families learned this the hard way after 2021, and now use fund relationships to reach direct co-investments instead.

Lesson · Ronald Diamond, Diamond Wealth

The direct-deal boom, and the retreat

Between the financial crisis and COVID, Diamond says, almost everything went up, so many families asked why they should pay a fund manager's fees when they could invest directly. Then rates rose, and the losses showed. "In venture capital in particular, you either do it full time or you don't do it, period," he says. Families are now outsourcing to specialists again, and in the Roundtable he called saving fees "never a reason to invest."

Framework · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Weigh risk against fees

Wendy ranks the three routes into venture by risk and cost. Families new to venture should start with funds, she says, because a bad first direct deal can put a family off the whole asset class: "if you go into a specific deal and you don't know what you're doing and you lose your shirt then you're soured on the entire asset class." In the Roundtable she was blunter: a family that has only ever invested in real estate has "absolutely no business doing a direct venture deal."

Funds of funds bring diversification, but "families are fees sensitive," Wendy says, and a second layer of fees has to be worth it.

Technique · Lara Nuchowicz, Slava Darkhaev and Vishal Verma

Use funds as a pipeline to co-investments

Several of the family office investors we spoke with back funds partly for what comes with them. Lara Nuchowicz, principal at AR Capital, a single-family office, says funds give the family exposure to hundreds of companies and "a network that we cannot replicate ourselves." Its direct deals are its highest-conviction bets, and they're usually sourced from the managers it backs.

Slava Darkhaev, head of VC allocations at a single-family office, picks funds first on whether they'll deliver returns. Getting into the best portfolio companies is then up to him: "it's my job, not their job to get me into the companies," by showing the manager what he understands and what he can add. Vishal Verma's family uses its relationships with the funds it backs to co-invest in their later-stage companies.

Warning · Ken Goldman, formerly Hillspire

Know what an SPV really gives you

Ken Goldman says some investors may not own what they think they own: an SPV can sit inside another SPV, giving you a derivative of the shares rather than the shares. And "you can't get liquidity until the whole SPV gets liquidity." His advice is to "beware when people think you're the dumb money."

He also notes that the managers families most want are often closed: the best venture and private equity firms have waiting lists because their existing investors re-up every time.

For fund managers

  • Explain what you give a family that it couldn't get investing directly.
  • Say upfront whether and how you offer co-investments.
  • If you run a fund of funds or SPV, show the full fee stack and structure.

For families

  • Invest directly only where you have expertise or a dedicated team.
  • Start with funds in any asset class that's new to you.
  • Before any SPV, find out exactly what you own and when you can sell.

Stage 5 of 7 · Who do we trust?

What do family offices look for in a venture fund manager?

Short answerExpertise and access the family can't get on its own, a network that leads to the best founders, honesty about what could go wrong, and incentives that line up with theirs.

Technique · Michael Wallach, Levy Family Partners

Sell the knowledge, not just the returns

The Levy family invests in funds to learn from specialists, Michael says. He asks whether a manager will "have access to deal flow that we don't have already," help the family vet companies it can't assess alone, and add to its picture of where the economy is heading.

Wendy adds a caution in the other direction: a thesis that's too narrow shrinks the number of families who can say yes. A focused fund that families can easily understand reaches more of them than a hyper-specific one.

Technique · Slava Darkhaev, Single-Family Office

Show why you, specifically, will win

Slava asks every manager two questions: why they do this, and why they'll win. The answer should come from lived experience, not be "made up for the pitch." For a fund manager, that usually means a track record of spotting great companies early and a place in the networks where the best founders are found. Because venture returns are concentrated in a few firms, he says, an emerging manager needs to be close to that circle, seeing companies before the biggest funds do.

Technique · Michael Wallach, Levy Family Partners

Name your risks before they do

The most common mistake managers make with families, Michael says, is too little humility. "Humility is really important in finding the right balance between believing in what you're doing, showing that you have an unfair advantage to win, but also acknowledging that the road ahead is very unlikely to be linear and without setbacks." Tell them what could go wrong and how you'll handle it.

He also wishes he'd paid more attention earlier to how managers and founders are really paid: whether the economics come at the end, alongside the family's, or accrue along the way.

Re-ups from families

Families notice when managers raise too often. Vishal says, "if I put ten dollars into a fund that's raising money every year, I will lower my allocation to the fund the next year." Funds that raise every two or three years, across market cycles, he still considers reasonable.

For fund managers

  • Lead with the expertise and access the family doesn't have.
  • Explain your edge from your own experience, with evidence.
  • Put your risks and your plan for them in the deck.
  • Show how you and your team are paid.

For families

  • Ask each manager what you'd learn from them.
  • Ask what could go wrong, and judge the answer.
  • Check how the manager's economics line up with yours.

Stage 6 of 7 · Who do we trust?

How do you find, approach and keep a family office as an LP?

Short answerExpect them to be hard to find. Do your research, get introduced by people they trust, lead with the relationship, accept a slower process, and never let them hear news from someone else first.

Lesson · Ronald Diamond and Anurag Chandra

They're hard to find on purpose

"You can go to PitchBook to find the top VC or PE firms, but you can't go to PitchBook to find the top family offices," Diamond says. Anurag notes that most family offices don't have websites and don't advertise for deal flow. That's a choice: privacy protects the family. Wendy Craft keeps her family's website deliberately blank.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Family Office Roundtable

Get referred by another family

Wendy gets most of her deal flow from people she has met and from other families, who often share their own diligence. Those referrals carry weight: "the ones I get internally take a lot less time," she says, because a sophisticated family office executive can explain exactly why a deal fits. Ken Goldman recommends conferences that ban soliciting outright: at the best ones, "if they find a sponsor soliciting money trying to get people to invest, they get kicked out of the conference." Families attend because they aren't pitched, so go to learn and meet people, not to sell.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

The outreach do's and don'ts

Teaching works, she says, because families rarely admit what they don't know but will show up to learn it: "if you teach they trust you and they'll invest with you." And speed works against you: "you cannot go to a family and say we're closing in 10 days would you like to write a check."

Anurag Chandra gives the same advice from the institutional side. Fundraising with "the Tin Cup out" sends the wrong signal; with LPs, "it's a long game it's a slow game." Start the relationship early, give them reasons to stay in touch, and expect the commitment to come in a later fund.

Qualify them too

Salvatore Buscemi warns there are also "fake families": people who call themselves a family office but invest like individual retail investors. He asks every family how it makes decisions and who else needs to be involved. A family with no answer may not be able to commit.

Technique · Michael Wallach and Wendy Craft

Keep them with no surprises

Once a family is in, the relationship runs on communication. Michael doesn't ask for weekly updates, but he expects to hear good or bad news from the manager first.

Lara says families want to be treated as more than capital: "we're not just a check, we're also resource," able to make introductions and co-invest. And when it works, Wendy says, families are loyal: "they'll stay with you through little ups and downs where an institution won't."

From VC10X

Teaching is one of the most effective ways to earn a family's trust, and a podcast does it at scale. A guest spot on someone else's show is borrowed attention; a show of your own builds a library of conversations that families can listen to before they ever take a meeting, in their own time and with no pitch attached. For a family that won't be cold-called, that is often the first introduction to how you think.

Prashant Choubey, Founder & Host of VC10X, also runs Podcast10x, which helps venture firms start and run their own podcast, so each recorded conversation also becomes LinkedIn posts, YouTube videos and email updates for LPs and founders.

For fund managers

  • Build a list of families through referrals and non-solicitation events, not cold lists.
  • Research each family's origin and generation before contact.
  • Offer education, not a pitch, in the first meeting.
  • Plan for a longer diligence process than with institutions.
  • Tell LPs news, good or bad, before anyone else does.

For families

  • Share diligence and deal flow with families you trust.
  • Tell managers what you invest in, so the wrong pitches stop.
  • Agree with each manager how and when you'll hear news.

Stage 7 of 7 · For families

How are the family offices that last run?

Short answerThey set up structure before they invest, keep a clear thesis, review what they own, hire people for the mission, put governance in place, and bring the next generation into the room.

Technique · Ronald Diamond, Family Office Roundtable

After a liquidity event, invest last

The day a sale is announced, everyone starts pitching. Diamond says the first thing to do is the boring work, and in his solo episode his advice is to "don't invest for 6 to 12 months."

Technique · John Messervey and Salvatore Buscemi

Have a thesis, and review what you own

John Messervey, who has advised more than 400 wealthy families since 1985, warns against "golf course investing": putting money into whatever someone suggests on the fourth hole until the portfolio is a collection of deals nobody understands. His rule, borrowed from Peter Lynch, is to "know what you own and why you own it," with "a very vigorous review every 12 or 18 months" of why each holding is still there, including the legacy business that hasn't performed in years.

Salvatore Buscemi says the most common mistake is whose advice families follow: "they follow people who are on the sell side they don't follow people who write checks."

Technique · Ken Goldman and Ronald Diamond

Hire for the mission, and pay to compete

A family office can't offer stock options. Ken says the answer is to "attract people that are not looking for the equity kick": people who want a mission, balance and a direct connection with the principals, backed by fair salaries and bonuses, good benefits and an open-door culture. Hillspire had almost no staff turnover in his five years. Diamond says investment roles are different: to win talent from private equity firms, families need to offer carry, co-investment and deal participation, and to see a good hire as a profit center rather than a cost.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Put governance in place before you need it

"All I think about every day is what can go wrong," Wendy says, and governance is where families most often slip. When busy principals hand responsibilities to staff without controls, "you can really end up being embezzled from." Safeguards around banking and approvals are no insult to a good executive: "no true executive is going to be offended by that." Families also write their values into a family constitution and trusts, and she notes they usually set rules about what heirs can't do, "a don't do instead of a have to do," because no one knows which investments will exist in 50 years.

Technique · Wendy Craft and Lara Nuchowicz

Bring the next generation into the room

Wendy's family brings its teenage daughter to every family office conference and seats her in the next-generation panels, and Wendy meets the next generation several times a week to teach them. She's frustrated by how rare that is.

Lara Nuchowicz is that next generation: she runs her family's private markets program while her father runs its hedge fund investments, and says families "are definitely driven by the next gen", who grew up more tech-native. Diamond adds that younger family members ask how a company treats its staff and the environment, and want philanthropy run more like a venture portfolio.

Do most families lose their wealth by the third generation?

The figure is everywhere. Diamond cites the common estimate that only 25 percent of families make it to the second generation, 10 percent to the third and 5 percent to the fourth. John Messervey disagrees: wealth changes shape, he says, as families sell businesses and change names, and the studies can't track it, calling them "pretty lousy research." Both agree on the cure: trust, structure and a prepared next generation.

For families

  • Set up estate planning and governance before investing a new liquidity event.
  • Write down your thesis, and review every holding every 12 to 18 months.
  • Put controls on banking and approvals, whoever runs the office.
  • Pay investment staff in a way that competes for talent.
  • Bring the next generation into meetings and conferences now.

What fund managers should know

  • A family with governance in place can commit faster and more reliably.
  • The next generation may be the decision-maker by your next fund.
  • Families value managers who help educate their heirs.

The Family Office checklist

Thirty-five steps drawn from this playbook, grouped by stage. Stages 1 to 6 are mostly for fund managers raising from families; stage 7 is for families. The PDF adds tick boxes and a one-page build-or-buy check for families.

VC10X · Family Offices

Stage 1: What kind of family office

  1. Identify the structure: inside the business, multi-family office or single-family office.
  2. Find out which generation is in charge.
  3. Find out who makes investment decisions, and whether an outsourced CIO is involved.
  4. Check whether the family invests in venture at all.
  5. Make your fit with their criteria clear on the first page of anything you send.

Stage 2: Where the money came from

  1. Research how the family made its money.
  2. List where your strategy fills a gap in their expertise.
  3. Avoid pitching families who invest directly in your sector.
  4. Prepare what you'd teach them about your area.
  5. Expect a concentrated core holding, and don't argue with it.

Stage 3: Taxes, liquidity and time

  1. Explain when and how the fund expects to return cash.
  2. Offer a path to earlier distributions if your strategy allows.
  3. Show the tax and filing burden for a taxable investor.
  4. State the fund term and any extension provisions plainly.
  5. Present returns after fees, and note what changes after tax.

Stage 4: Direct, funds or funds of funds

  1. Explain what the family gets from you that it can't get directly.
  2. Say whether and how you offer co-investments.
  3. Show the full fee stack for any fund of funds or SPV.
  4. Explain exactly what an SPV investor owns and when they can sell.
  5. Share how you add value to the founders you back.

Stage 5: What they want in a manager

  1. Lead with expertise and access the family doesn't have.
  2. Explain your edge from your own experience, with evidence.
  3. Name the risks, and your plan for each.
  4. Show how you and your team are paid.
  5. Explain how often you plan to raise, and why.

Stage 6: Find, approach and keep them

  1. Get introduced through another family or someone they trust.
  2. Attend events that ban soliciting, to learn and meet people.
  3. Don't send decks in LinkedIn messages, or ask about AUM or check size.
  4. Allow for a longer diligence process than with institutions.
  5. Tell families news, good or bad, before they hear it elsewhere.

Stage 7: Inside the family office (for families)

  1. After a liquidity event, set up estate planning and governance before investing.
  2. Write down the family's investment thesis.
  3. Review every holding and why you own it every 12 to 18 months.
  4. Put controls on banking and approvals.
  5. Bring the next generation into meetings and conferences.

Frequently asked questions

How much do family offices allocate to venture capital?
It varies widely. Ronald Diamond of Diamond Wealth, who invests alongside more than 100 family offices, says venture is typically 5 to 10 percent of a family office portfolio, and up to 25 percent for families that made their money in technology.
Do family offices invest directly in startups or through venture funds?
Both, but most should use funds. Ronald Diamond says many families that invested directly during the low-rate years lost money and are now outsourcing to specialists. Wendy Craft of Elle Family Office says families new to venture should start with funds. Families with expertise, such as AR Capital's Lara Nuchowicz, use funds to reach direct co-investments.
How do I find family offices to invest in my fund?
Mostly through introductions. Family offices rarely have websites or public mandates. Wendy Craft gets most of her deal flow from other families and people she has met, and Ken Goldman recommends conferences that ban soliciting. Research each family's origin and generation before reaching out.
What's the difference between a single-family office and a multi-family office?
A single-family office serves one family with dedicated staff and full privacy, at full cost. A multi-family office shares staff and expertise across many families. Ronald Diamond says a single-family office needs at least $250 million, and in our roundtable put the figure nearer $500 million, to make economic sense.
How long does a family office take to commit to a fund?
Longer than most institutions. Wendy Craft says a family's discussions and diligence will take longer than a 10-day close, and Lara Nuchowicz says it's "not overnight." Building the relationship before you raise helps.
Why do family offices care so much about taxes and liquidity?
Because, unlike pensions and endowments, they pay taxes and fund their own expenses. Anurag Chandra says his family office picks up alpha through tax optimization, and Wendy Craft says families can't afford to be tied up for 12 years.
