The six questions behind a family office that lasts

Most family offices are young. Ronald Diamond, founder of Diamond Wealth, who has invested alongside more than 100 family offices for over 20 years, says most were started after 2000, and that there is still no standard playbook for running one. Every family is different, but the executives, principals and advisors we interviewed keep returning to the same six questions.

Getting them right matters more than any single investment. As Dave White, founder of Wayfinder Advisors and a former managing director at Cambridge Associates, puts it, if the portfolio is the house, "the foundation is going to be your governance and your operations."

Stage 1 of 6 · Do we need a family office?

Do you need a family office, and what kind?

Short answerProbably not a single-family office of your own unless you have at least a few hundred million dollars and a reason beyond status. Below that, a multi-family office or an outsourced CIO usually does the job better and cheaper. Start by asking why.

Framework · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Know the three structures

Wendy Craft, CEO of Elle Family Office, a single-family office in Atlanta, describes three ways families organize, each with a different trade-off between cost, privacy and expertise. Running the family's affairs out of the operating business is cheapest, but it means "you're asking things of your staff that you shouldn't be asking because they're not trained in it": a company CFO who keeps excellent books may know nothing about alternative investments, and employees see every purchase and charity check.

A multi-family office can pay bills and run investments, though most won't do legal work, and with it "you are not getting complete privacy." A single-family office gives full privacy and dedicated staff, yet "even most single family offices will outsource some aspects," such as tax returns across many states. Many families combine them: their own office for what they know, and a multi-family office for asset classes they don't.

Lesson · Ronald Diamond, Diamond Wealth

Know the size that makes it worth it

Diamond says that "in order to have a single family office make economic sense, you need a bare-bones minimum of $250 million." In our Family Office Roundtable he set the bar higher: "you need about 500 million." Below that, splitting administrative, legal and tax costs across 20 families in a multi-family office is cheaper. He goes further: "85 to 90% of the family offices that exist shouldn't exist," and "I think it's driven primarily by ego."

Wendy gets the same question all the time. "My first question is why," she says. In most cases, she says, it's more efficient to outsource to a multi-family office: it frees up the family's time and funds. A family office of your own makes sense when the family needs that level of privacy, sophistication and dedication. Otherwise: "for so many people, please just go to an MFO."

Trend · Wendy Craft and Ronald Diamond, Family Office Roundtable

Choose between a multi-family office and an outsourced CIO

Ahead of the wealth transfer, Wendy says several first-generation families she knows have handed their investments to outsourced CIO firms with 40 or 50 staff, while keeping the trusts, estates and foundation in-house. They are confident they can run the family side, but not that they "can pick the deals or do the things that the first gen did."

Diamond draws the line between the two options. An outsourced CIO only invests. Multi-family offices "are much more holistic," covering bill pay, governance, succession and next-generation work as well. And as the two of them explain, multi-family offices come in two kinds: bank-run offices that put you in their own products, and open-architecture offices that pick outside managers for a fiduciary fee. A family that already has its own estate and administration team, like Wendy's, may only need the investment piece.

For families

  • Write down why you want a family office before you set one up.
  • Compare the cost with a multi-family office or outsourced CIO at your size.
  • Decide how much privacy you need, and what you'll pay for it.

For family office executives

  • Map what the family needs: investments, administration, legal, tax, governance.
  • Decide which of those the office does itself and which it buys.
  • If you use a multi-family office, know whether it's open architecture or sells its own products.

Stage 2 of 6 · What comes first?

What should a family do first after a liquidity event?

Short answerNot invest. Put the estate plan, governance and succession in place, decide what the wealth is for, and write down an investment thesis. Then invest, slowly, in what you understand.

Technique · Ronald Diamond, Diamond Wealth

Invest last

The day a sale is announced, everyone starts pitching. The instinct is to start investing right away. Diamond says that's the worst thing to do: "don't invest for 6 to 12 months." First, build the structure, starting with an estate planning attorney. Most families get the order wrong: "95% of families do it in reverse."

The second reason offices fail, he says, is "the ego of the founder, patriarch, or matriarch." Building one business well doesn't transfer to every asset class: "Making a billion dollars selling widgets doesn't mean you're good at SaaS."

Technique · Dave White and Salvatore Buscemi

Decide what the wealth is for

When a founder has just sold a company, Dave's first conversation isn't about venture funds or private equity. Once a portfolio is built it's like steering a cruise ship, hard to turn quickly, so the family has to agree on the purpose first. Portfolio construction, liquidity and how aggressive to be all follow from it: "it really starts up front with the purpose."

Salvatore Buscemi, managing partner at Brahmin Partners, which works with some of the wealthiest families, starts with a legacy plan and an impact statement. Families often don't know what matters to them, so he works from the other side: "talk about things that you are not into." Ruling things out makes the rest clearer.

Technique · John Messervey, Salvatore Buscemi and Aoifinn Devitt

Have a thesis, and avoid golf course investing

John Messervey, who has advised more than 400 wealthy families since 1985, warns against "golf course investing": you've just sold the family business, someone suggests a deal on the fourth hole, another on the sixth, and before long the portfolio is full of investments nobody understands. "You really have to stick to your knitting," he says, and know the areas the family understands well.

His second rule, borrowed from Peter Lynch, is to "know what you own and why you own it," with "a very vigorous review every 12 or 18 months." That includes the legacy business the family keeps because grandpa started it, "which hasn't done anything in years except being a drag on the boat." And he passes on an economist's advice: avoid one large loss that takes years to recover from, because good investing is "long slow and steady."

Buscemi says the most common mistake is whose advice families take: "they follow people who are on the sell side they don't follow people who write checks." Aoifinn Devitt, managing director of global wealth at Moneta Group, adds a behavioral check: clients "seem to be fine on the up markets, but they get very skittish" when markets turn. For a family member who does want to speculate, her firm ring-fences a small "play portfolio" with a set limit, rather than letting speculation spread through everything.

For families

  • Don't invest a new liquidity event for 6 to 12 months.
  • See an estate planning attorney first, then set up governance.
  • Write down what the wealth is for, and what you won't invest in.
  • Take advice from people who write checks, not people selling.

For family office executives

  • Write the family's investment thesis down, and keep to it.
  • Review every holding, including the legacy business, every 12 to 18 months.
  • Ring-fence any speculative money with a set limit.

Stage 3 of 6 · Who runs it?

Who should run a family office, and what should it outsource?

Short answerKeep in-house what the family is best at and needs to keep private, and outsource the rest. Hire people who want a mission and balance rather than equity, pay investment staff like a firm that wants to win, and use AI to stay small.

Lesson · Ken Goldman, formerly Hillspire

Run it with discipline, but it isn't a corporation

Ken Goldman ran the top end. As president of Hillspire, Eric Schmidt's family office, he oversaw more than 500 people. Everything to do with the family was in-house: accounting for the investments, legal work and estates ("all that work was done internally"), plus properties, aviation, and the crews of a research vessel. He brought in corporate discipline: goals and objectives, performance reviews, a new IT department, weekly staff meetings, all-hands meetings, and "a very robust financial package that we reviewed with Eric monthly."

But he warns against running it like a company. "You have to realize it isn't a corporation," he says. There's no conventional hierarchy, and people work there for different reasons. You have to understand what matters to the principals, and in a multigenerational family, to each generation.

Technique · Ken Goldman and Ronald Diamond

Hire for the mission, and pay to compete

A family office can't offer stock options. Ken's answer is to look for a different kind of person.

"Are you going to get the best AI people? Probably not," he admits. Instead he hired people who wanted a mission, balance and a direct connection with the principals, and backed that with fair salaries and bonuses, good benefits, time off and an open-door culture. "It has to be a great place to work," he says, and Hillspire "had almost zero attrition."

Investment roles are different. Diamond says families undervalue them: "If a family office pays a Stanford grad $250k, they often view it as a $250k cost. If KKR or Blackstone pays that, they view the hire as a potential $2 million profit center." To win talent from private equity firms, families need to offer "carry, co-investment, lines of credit, and deal participation." John Messervey says the strongest offices he has seen are built on "the ability to go out and find non-family talent."

Technique · Ronald Diamond and Wendy Craft, Family Office Roundtable

Do what you're good at, and outsource the rest

Most offices can't do everything. "You have to figure out what you're good at and what you enjoy, and then the rest you can outsource," Diamond says. Wendy is blunter about small offices of 10 to 15 people: "you can't be an expert in every single asset and if you think you can be you're a fool." A common pattern is to run the asset class the family made its money in, and hand the rest to a multi-family office or outsourced CIO.

Trend · Wendy Craft, Ronald Diamond and Sinéad Colton Grant

Let AI do the analyst work, not the deciding

Smaller offices are replacing analysts with software. "At least four of the family offices I know down here no longer have an analyst," Wendy says. Work that took an analyst three weeks now takes minutes, though someone still has to check it, and the saving goes beyond salary to benefits, insurance and retirement plans. Diamond says AI will screen deals against a family's criteria, but "there will always be a human in the loop" to make the final call.

Sinéad Colton Grant, former CIO of BNY Wealth, notes that there are "many family offices deploying significant capital that have fewer than ten people on staff," which is why the efficiency is so attractive. Her caution: test it thoroughly first, and make sure "you understand what the cyber implications are."

For families

  • Decide what stays in-house and what's outsourced, and review it as the office grows.
  • Meet the office's leaders regularly, and review the finances monthly.
  • Pay investment staff in a way that competes for talent.

For family office executives

  • Hire people who want the mission and balance, not equity.
  • Bring in goals, reviews and regular all-hands meetings, without forcing a corporate hierarchy.
  • Test any AI tool for accuracy and cyber risk before relying on it.

Stage 4 of 6 · What could go wrong?

How do family offices protect the family and its wealth?

Short answerAssume things will go wrong and plan for them: controls on money and approvals, trained staff who act as gatekeepers, a clear line between what is public and what is private, and a family that stays quiet about its wealth.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Put controls in place before you need them

"All I think about every day is what can go wrong," Wendy says: the trust and the will in case someone dies, insurance, and what the portfolio is hedging against. Governance is where families most often slip. Busy principals hand responsibilities to staff, and without rails in place, "you can really end up being embezzled from." That "hurts trust and that hurts the heart," not just the balance sheet.

Safeguards on banking information and approvals protect the executive too. Families also assume their children will never divorce, gamble or develop an addiction, so why go to the trouble? Because "they're perfect till they're not perfect." Her advice: "let your executive be the little cloud of doom so that you can have a great life." She compares a good family office to a house built for hurricanes and fires: "that's the family office you want the house it's still standing."

Technique · Wendy Craft and John Messervey

Train staff as gatekeepers, and keep a public and a private story

"If you're a family office and very wealthy you're already a target," Wendy says. Her example, told to her by a security firm: criminals in Westchester noted family names from plaques on cars at a private school, called the homes during the school's published spring break posing as the school, and household staff helpfully told them where the families had gone. The homes were robbed. Household staff have to be trained "to both protect and be a gatekeeper" as well as to serve.

The same goes for health records, travel itineraries and anything posted online. Her family's website "is purposely blank." For a family that can't stay out of the news, she recommends "your public story and your private story": share the family's history and community work, never the children's names, where they live, the net worth or where the money sits. The family and its executive agree where that line is.

John Messervey's advice to the next generation is the same: "you've got to be very quiet about your wealth." Posting a nightclub bill on social media doesn't sit well with the workers at the family's plant. He also recommends something many families skip: "background checks on your in-laws."

Lesson · Salvatore Buscemi and Dave White

Treat governance as the foundation

For older families, Buscemi says, governance is "the cement that keeps everything together." It's also how he tells real families from fake ones: when he doesn't know a family office, he asks "what's your governance like," how it makes investment decisions, and who else has to sign off. Dave White defines risk not as volatility but as the permanent loss of capital, and says families focused on returns while underinvesting in governance face consequences that "are immense."

For families

  • Put controls on banking and approvals, whoever runs the office.
  • Agree with your executive what's public and what stays private.
  • Keep wealth off social media, and teach the next generation to do the same.

For family office executives

  • List what could go wrong, and plan for each.
  • Train household staff as gatekeepers.
  • Protect health records, travel plans and the family's details online.

Stage 5 of 6 · How do we decide together?

How should a family make decisions together?

Short answerWrite the rules down: a mission statement, a constitution and trusts that say what heirs can't do. Expect conflict, and handle it early with a neutral mediator. Budget for giving like any other line.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Elle Family Office

Write a constitution, and make the rules "don't do"

Wendy points families to two documents: a family mission statement, and a family constitution. The constitution answers questions such as whether spouses get a full say in the family office, whether adopted children are treated the same as natural children, and what would cost someone their place as a beneficiary. Like a country's constitution, it can be amended, but it should be hard to do.

Families usually write rules about what heirs can't do rather than what they must do, "a don't do instead of a have to do," because no one knows which investments will exist in 50 years. Trusts carry the rules too: a dynastic trust can say "you will not be able to access this money until you are 25," and that the money is to be stewarded, "not just for you to go buy a yacht." The wealthiest families are increasingly setting up private trust companies of their own. Children should learn the rules young, at 15 or 16.

Technique · John Messervey, Family Wealth Advisor

Expect conflict, and deal with it early

Every family has differences, Messervey says, and money "adds an exponent to conflict." Trust comes first: "if you don't have trust you really shouldn't be in business together." When disputes come, he recommends a neutral, trusted mediator, not lawyers: "attorneys are just awful at this." He teaches a few openings that lower the temperature: "help me understand," and "when you said this I felt this," because no one can tell you you're wrong to feel something.

Some disagreements are genuine and won't be resolved; the skill is accepting that and moving on. He is also a strong advocate of prenuptial agreements: he says easily 90 percent of new marriages among his client families now require them, as a family-wide rule rather than a judgment on one in-law. Both sides should have their own counsel, and the agreement should be signed months ahead, "never sign it on the Thursday before the weekend wedding."

Technique · Ken Goldman, formerly Hillspire

Budget for giving every year

At Hillspire, philanthropy was part of the annual budget: "we would allocate every year how much we wanted to put into philanthropy." Two foundations reflected each principal's interests, one technology and one the environment, with a board reviewing where the money went and how it was used. The foundations' teams made decisions up to a set amount; above it, the principals approved. Ken says there's no right percentage; it depends on what the family needs to live on and wants to invest.

For families

  • Write a family mission statement and constitution.
  • Put "don't do" rules and access ages into trusts.
  • Agree on prenuptial agreements as a family-wide rule, signed months ahead.

For family office executives

  • Keep the constitution current, with a clear process for amendments.
  • Bring in a neutral mediator early when disputes start.
  • Set an annual giving budget, with approval limits.

Stage 6 of 6 · Who comes next?

How do families prepare the next generation?

Short answerStart early and include them: teach stewardship, tell the family's real story, bring them into meetings, and give them something of their own to run. Share values long before you share the number.

Technique · Wendy Craft, Family Office Roundtable

Bring the next generation into the room

"We educate our nextgens every single day," Wendy says. She meets the family's next generation several times a week to explain financial terms and what it means to be a trust beneficiary, and has them practice on trading platforms. Her family takes one of its daughters to every family office conference and seats her in the next-generation panels. She's frustrated by how rare that is.

"You can't prepare them for this if they're not included in the discussion," she says. Families that last, in her view, start "training your children at a very young age about financial stewardship."

Technique · Salvatore Buscemi and John Messervey

Share values before the number

Buscemi's advice is to talk to children early about values, but not the figure: "if you let that number fly out of your mouth you could never get it back." Families should "let them into the conversations earlier," and find out whether a child actually wants to be a steward. Many don't, and "would rather just ski in Colorado," in which case a multi-family office may be the answer.

Messervey says there's no single right age, because every child matures differently, but "I wouldn't do it before 18." Parents model the behavior all the time. He wants the next generation to hear the real story of how the wealth was made, with its "fits and starts lots of failures lots of close calls," and some families now record the oldest generation's recollections on video. His message to heirs: "your job is to add value solve problems and work effectively." And he warns that by about 40, an heir in the family business will ask "when is it my turn."

Technique · Dave White, Lara Nuchowicz and Sinéad Colton Grant

Give them agency, and something of their own

Dave says what most helps wealth endure is "providing the next generation with some sort of agency." Some families keep everything hidden until a set age; others put "three generations at the table," explaining things for the 13-year-old while the 75-year-old leads. Either way, make it relatable: someone into fitness might meet the investor behind a fitness company they use. Some families carve out part of the portfolio for the next generation to run.

Lara Nuchowicz, principal at AR Capital, a single-family office, is that next generation. Her father took her to meetings with fund managers from age 15, and after college "my father gave me some capital to deploy." Today she runs the family's private markets program while he runs its hedge fund investments. "Families are definitely driven by the next gen," she says, and they should give heirs room to build their own things, because "you don't want to end up having no one at the end of it." Sinéad says sports investing is one area that draws the next generation in.

Do most families lose their wealth by the third generation?

The figure is everywhere. Diamond cites the common estimate that only 25 percent of families make it to the second generation, 10 percent to the third and 5 percent to the fourth. Buscemi says the wealth is usually lost "because there's no governance that's been put together," and Dave says the third generation is "far enough removed that they take things for granted." John Messervey disagrees with the statistic itself: wealth changes shape, he says, as families sell businesses and change names, and the studies can't track it, calling them "pretty lousy research" that helps "sell a lot of life insurance." All of them agree on what helps: trust, structure and a prepared next generation.

For families

  • Teach stewardship young, and tell the real story of how the wealth was made.
  • Share values early; share the number only when a child is ready, not before 18.
  • Bring the next generation into meetings and conferences now.
  • Give them something of their own to run.

For family office executives

  • Run a regular education program for the next generation.
  • Find out early who wants to be a steward, and plan for those who don't.
  • Record the family's history while the founders can tell it.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do you need to start a single-family office?
Ronald Diamond of Diamond Wealth says a single-family office needs a bare-bones minimum of $250 million to make economic sense, and in our Family Office Roundtable put the figure nearer $500 million. Below that, he says, a multi-family office that splits administrative, legal and tax costs across many families is more economical.
What's the difference between a single-family office, a multi-family office and an outsourced CIO?
A single-family office serves one family with dedicated staff and full privacy, at full cost. A multi-family office shares staff and expertise across many families and can cover bill pay, governance and succession as well as investments. An outsourced CIO only manages investments. Wendy Craft of Elle Family Office says many first-generation families now hand their investments to outsourced CIO firms while keeping trusts and estates in-house.
What should a family do first after selling a business?
Not invest. Ronald Diamond advises waiting 6 to 12 months, starting with an estate planning attorney, then governance, succession and the next generation, and only then investing. Dave White of Wayfinder Advisors says the first conversation should be about what the wealth is for.
What is a family constitution?
A written document setting out how the family makes decisions and what is expected of its members, such as whether spouses have a say and what would cost someone their place as a beneficiary. Wendy Craft says families usually write rules about what heirs can't do, rather than what they must do, and amend the constitution as times change.
When should parents tell children about the family's wealth?
There's no single age. John Messervey, who has advised more than 400 families, says it depends on each child's maturity, but he wouldn't do it before 18. Salvatore Buscemi of Brahmin Partners recommends teaching values early but not mentioning a number, because once it's said it can't be taken back.
Do most families really lose their wealth by the third generation?
It's a common claim. Ronald Diamond cites estimates that only 25 percent of families make it to the second generation and 10 percent to the third. John Messervey disputes the research, saying wealth changes shape as families sell businesses and change names, and the studies can't track it.
