The seven stages at a glance

A first fund is a bet on a person and a plan. Every fund after it is a test of whether the plan happened. The managers we interviewed have raised second, third and eighth funds, and the allocators have sat through hundreds of re-up decisions. They describe the same work, most of which happens long before the next raise starts.

The odds of a next fund

Short answerMost first funds never get a successor. Fund II is usually judged on how the portfolio is marking up; by Fund III, LPs want to see cash coming back.

Matt Curtolo, a former institutional allocator, cites one GP's count that of more than 900 Fund Is raised in 2021 and 2022, only about 200 had raised a successor fund by early 2026. David Hornik, founding partner of Lobby Capital, closed his second fund in what he calls one of the hardest markets he has seen: by his account, "three out of four firms that were out raising last year did not end up raising a fund."

What LPs ask for changes with each fund. Marlon Nichols, managing general partner of MaC Venture Capital, has raised three funds and describes the shift. Fund II comes so soon after Fund I that "LPs are not really looking for DPI, right? They are looking for TVPI," along with how the underlying companies are performing. By Fund III, more time has passed, so "DPI starts to matter more." In a slow exit market, that makes Fund III the harder raise.

Returns come first, and no technique replaces them. But returns take years to show, and the managers who make it to Fund III do several other things well in the meantime. That's what the seven stages cover.

Stage 1 of 7 · During Fund I

When does the Fund II raise start?

Short answerThe day Fund I closes. Use Fund I to collect the evidence Fund II will need, keep LPs who passed in the loop, and plan for the LPs who backed Fund I but can't grow with you.

A second raise is easier when the LPs you need have watched you work for two or three years. The managers we spoke with treated Fund I as the first chapter of a longer pitch.

Technique · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Build Fund I as the evidence for Fund II

Kevin sized his first fund to prove three things: the founders he backs, how he adds value and how the firm runs. The goal was a platform he could scale, so that when he went out for the next fund he'd have evidence of what he'd done right.

Several LPs passed on Fund I. The feedback that stayed with him: right-size the fund, build the track record, stay in touch and come back for Fund II. One of those LPs told him that taking the advice "shows a lot of maturity."

Technique · Kevin Moore, Serac Ventures

Meet your Fund III LPs now

Kevin expects it to take 12 to 18 months for an LP he doesn't already know to consider him, and longer for large institutions. So he keeps them updated long before he needs them.

Technique · Marlon Nichols, MaC Venture Capital

Bring in institutions early, if your record allows it

MaC raised from institutional LPs from its first fund, Marlon says, because it gave the firm "an easier path to future fundraisers" and let the partners learn what an institutional-grade firm looks like. Institutions think in multiple funds.

It's not the usual path. Marlon and his partners had already run small proof-of-concept funds before MaC's Fund I, so they arrived with track records and relationships. Most first-time managers raise from individuals, family offices and funds of funds instead, as Playbook #2 covers.

Lesson · Jonathan Lacoste and Jenny Fielding

Plan for Fund I LPs who can't scale with you

Loyal Fund I LPs are often too small to fill a bigger Fund II. Jonathan Lacoste of Ambition Ventures says his Fund I investors kept supporting him, but nearly all of his second fund, 98 percent by his count, was new capital, including several institutional funds of funds that could grow with the firm.

Jenny Fielding of Everywhere Ventures faced it from the other side. Her first two funds were raised entirely from founders and operators. By Fund III, the fund was too big for that many small checks, so the firm added family offices, funds of funds and a few later-stage venture funds, while keeping its 500 founder and operator LPs at the core.

Do this

  • Write down what Fund I must prove for Fund II, and track it every quarter.
  • Keep a list of LPs who passed or were too big for Fund I, and update them.
  • Estimate how much of Fund II your Fund I LPs can realistically cover.

What LPs will check

  • Whether you stayed in touch after they passed.
  • Whether your Fund I LPs are re-upping.
  • Whether the firm can run at the next fund's scale.

Stage 2 of 7 · Keep your promises

What do LPs check before they re-up?

Short answerWhether the fund you built is the fund you sold: the number of companies, the ownership, the sectors and the check sizes. Change the strategy only with a reason LPs can follow, and say what gives you the right to win somewhere new.

An LP's re-up decision starts from the pitch you gave them last time. They built your fund into their own portfolio plan, so drift doesn't just disappoint them; it breaks their model.

Technique · Marlon Nichols, MaC Venture Capital

Deliver the car you sold

If MaC said it would invest in 36 to 40 companies, Marlon says, it invested in 36 to 40 companies, and it hit the ownership target it promised. LPs are doing their own portfolio construction, and a fund that arrives different from the pitch leaves a gap in it.

Technique · TX Zhuo, Fika Ventures

Set targets for every fund, then report against them

"Obviously performance matters," says TX Zhuo, GP at Fika Ventures, but "more importantly is being accountable for what you would say you would do." For every new fund, Fika sets its goals in writing:

  • The ownership target in every deal.
  • The verticals it will double down on.
  • The investments it will avoid.

Sticking to them, and showing LPs that it has, is how Fika builds trust between funds, he says.

Technique · Morgan Flager and Neil Sequeira

Change the strategy only with a reason LPs can follow

Strategies do move. Morgan Flager, managing partner of Silverton Partners, is raising the firm's eighth fund. LPs now ask far more about how changes in software affect the strategy, he says, and if a firm moves into areas next to what it has done before, they ask "what gives you the right to win in those categories and how are you going to get access to those deals."

Neil Sequeira, founding GP of Defy, changed portfolio construction between funds: AI companies often need smaller first checks now, so Fund III will hold more companies. His LPs, mostly endowments and foundations, accepted it. "They want you to kind of adjust with the market," he says. What he doesn't do is follow what's hot.

Lesson · David Hornik, Lobby Capital

Drop the strategies you aren't built for

Earlier in his career, David's firm ran a special opportunities fund for late-stage deals alongside its core fund. Asked whether he'd do it again, he said no, "mostly because it's not my business." Lobby Capital invests early and in a concentrated way, and leaves later rounds to investors set up for them.

Where LPs see drift

Lara Nuchowicz of AR Capital, a single-family office, backs smaller managers partly because big-name firms become platforms that do many things and "get a little bit further away from their original thesis." Iren Reznikov of Vintage Investment Partners says few re-ups are a clean yes on every question: the fund may be "a bit bigger than what we wanted," there may be friction in the team, or capacity may be stretched.

Do this

  • Write down each fund's promises: company count, ownership, check sizes, sectors, what you'll avoid.
  • Report against them in your annual letter.
  • Before changing the strategy, write the one-paragraph case for why you'll win there.

What LPs will check

  • Your actual portfolio against the original pitch.
  • Whether any change was explained before or after it happened.
  • Whether the team is stretched.

LPs test this directly. See How do LPs decide whether to re-up? in How LPs Evaluate Venture Fund Managers.

Stage 3 of 7 · Between funds

How do you keep LPs close between funds?

Short answerSend reports on time, every quarter. Give your biggest LPs more of your time, tell them bad news before they hear it elsewhere, and see them in person.

Between raises, reporting is most of what an LP sees of you. The GPs we spoke with who keep their LPs fund after fund treat it as part of the job, not a compliance chore.

Technique · Brad Harrison, Scout Ventures

Report on time, and get ahead of bad news

Brad Harrison, founding GP of Scout Ventures, whose LPs have backed several funds, says "the most important thing is timely quarterly newsletters and reporting timely. Can't be late." His checklist:

  1. Send quarterly reports and newsletters on schedule.
  2. Avoid portfolio investments structured as LLCs: "you're always going to be late with your K1s every year."
  3. Add a tailored narrative for your largest LPs.
  4. When something goes wrong, tell the LP yourself, before they hear it elsewhere.

Matt adds that the best quarterly reports explain material changes at each company, not just the numbers, and that an annual meeting, quarterly calls or a webinar keep LPs feeling they're in the loop.

Technique · Brad Harrison, Scout Ventures

Make face time a habit

When Brad plans a trip, he checks which LPs are in that city and tries to see them for an update. Text and email lose too much, he says.

Technique · Matt Curtolo and Hunter Somerville

Keep telling your story, one to many

Quarterly reports cover the numbers. Matt says content LPs can follow alongside the financial statements gives "the opportunity to continue to tell your story": what you're seeing in the market, why you've sped up or slowed down, what you expect from a company. It reaches every LP at once, and prospective LPs for the next fund too. Hunter draws the line on quality: he doesn't want managers posting everywhere just to stay relevant, but "having really good content thoughtfully put together is something we appreciate more."

From VC10X

A podcast of your own is one way to do this, and it says something different from appearing on other people's shows. A guest spot is borrowed attention: someone else's audience, on their schedule, gone after one episode. Owned media builds up over time. Every conversation stays in a library that's yours, and the audience it gathers stays with the firm. To an LP weighing Fund III, a show that has run for years is visible evidence that you're building a platform and a brand for the long term, not just raising the next fund.

Lesson · Morgan Flager, Silverton Partners

Credibility on liquidity has to be earned

LPs have been asking about liquidity since 2022 and 2023, Morgan says, because many expected money back by now and haven't had it. After 20 years and distributions from earlier funds, Silverton can tell LPs what's coming and be believed. Younger firms have a harder time.

Brad's answer for long-dated funds is to plan for some liquidity early: Scout aims to sell some holdings in years five to seven of a fund, so LPs have DPI before any extension, and charges no or very low fees during extensions.

Do this

  • Set reporting dates for the year and treat them as fixed.
  • Write a short tailored note for each of your five largest LPs every quarter.
  • Plan in-person LP meetings around your travel.

What LPs will check

  • Whether reports and K-1s arrived on time.
  • Whether they heard bad news from you first.
  • Your plan for getting cash back to them.

Stage 4 of 7 · Size the next fund

How big should Fund II be?

Short answerWork it out from the strategy: how many companies, how much you need to own, how much you keep back for follow-ons. Grow only as far as the strategy still works, because fund size changes what you can invest in.

Demand for a second fund can run ahead of the strategy. LPs who see a hot Fund I may offer more than the strategy can absorb, and the extra capital pushes you into bigger checks, later rounds and lower ownership.

Technique · Marlon Nichols, MaC Venture Capital

Work backwards from the portfolio

Marlon builds the fund size from the bottom up:

  1. Set the number of companies. MaC targets 36 to 40, down from 50 in its first fund.
  2. Look at round sizes at your stage. Seed rounds, he says, now average five to seven million dollars.
  3. Work out the ownership you can get. CEOs typically sell 15 to 20 percent at seed; MaC targets about 10 percent.
  4. Add reserves. MaC keeps 40 percent of the fund for follow-ons in its best companies.

Rule of thumb · Hunter Somerville, StepStone Group

Bigger funds need higher ownership

Hunter Somerville, a partner at StepStone Group, expects a seed fund on its second, third or fourth fund that has grown to $100 to $200 million to own around 10 percent of its companies. "You have to earn the right to get that though," he adds, by being genuinely useful to founders before the multi-stage firms arrive.

Lesson · Jonathan Lacoste and Jenny Fielding

The capital you raise sets the strategy

"The capital you raise will dictate what your strategy is," Jonathan says: it decides how large a check you write and how you follow on. Jenny makes the same point from the small end. Everywhere Ventures expects about 90 small pre-seed investments in its current fund, and its fund size fits that strategy.

Size and returns

"Size is the enemy of returns," says Iren Reznikov, whose firm allocates to established and emerging managers alike. Lara Nuchowicz prefers funds under $100 million, and better still under $50 million, because smaller funds can return bigger multiples. Neither rules out large funds; both want a reason for the size.

Do this

  • Rebuild the fund size from company count, ownership and reserves.
  • Check what the new size does to your check sizes and entry stage.
  • Decide in advance how much demand above target you'll turn away.

What LPs will check

  • Whether the step-up in size fits the strategy.
  • Ownership in Fund I against what you'll need in Fund II.
  • Whether you're moving later to deploy more capital.

Stage 5 of 7 · Run the raise

How is a Fund II raise different?

Short answerYou start with LPs who already know you, and you have a portfolio to show. Keep the process tight so you're not out of the market for long, and close at your target rather than holding the fund open.

A first raise is a vision pitch. Jonathan compares raising Funds I, II and III to "raising maybe like a pre-seed or a seed round as a founder": some proof, but not years of fine-tuned metrics. What changes in Fund II is that your Fund I companies now do some of the talking.

Technique · Jonathan Lacoste, Ambition Ventures

Run a tight process

Many first funds invest and raise at the same time, which stretches the timeline. For his second fund, Jonathan set out to compress it, and closed in just under five months, against the 12 to 18 months he says most funds take.

Lesson · Marlon Nichols, MaC Venture Capital

Start with the LPs you have, and expect the market to set the pace

MaC's three raises took very different times. Fund I took 18 to 24 months, helped over the line by strong markups. Fund II took about six months in 2022, when, as Marlon puts it, cash was basically free. Fund III took about nine: the market had tightened, but existing institutions came back, some doubling or tripling their commitments.

Because existing LPs took so much of Fund III, MaC couldn't bring in the new institutions it wanted. Having hit its target, it chose not to keep the fund open longer to make room for them.

Lesson · David Hornik, Lobby Capital

Know which LP pockets are open

Usually some group of LPs is flush and active while others hold back, David says: foundations, university endowments, funds of funds or family offices. In his latest raise, none of them were. Some questioned venture as an asset class, others were overweight after committing heavily to large multi-stage funds. His core existing investors came back in, and that carried the fund.

Do this

  • Talk to every existing LP about their re-up before you announce the raise.
  • Set a target date to close and plan the raise to fit it.
  • Decide what you'll do if existing LPs want more than you can give.

What LPs will check

  • How many existing LPs are re-upping, and at what size.
  • How Fund I companies are performing.
  • Whether the raise is distracting you from investing.

Stage 6 of 7 · Grow the team

Who should you hire as the firm grows?

Short answerHire juniors you can train rather than senior people from other firms, add partners slowly, and invest in operations before the portfolio outgrows them. Expect fees to cover less than you'd like for several funds.

Hiring is where many second and third funds go wrong. A small team that worked well in Fund I can lose that with one wrong addition, and LPs know it.

Technique · Hunter Somerville, StepStone Group

Hire juniors and train them

Asked for the most common mistakes in Funds I and II, Hunter named hiring. Managers want to move fast and bring in experienced investors from other firms, who arrive with their own habits and their own view of culture.

He prefers promising younger hires at principal level who are trained and mentored inside the firm. Adding a second or third partner can disrupt a small team's chemistry, he warns; it happened at one manager StepStone backed, and it destroyed the firm.

Lesson · Brad Harrison, Scout Ventures

Put the fees into people

In Scout's early years, Brad's first colleagues earned more than he did; he lived on income from an earlier business. Management fees on a small fund don't stretch far, he warns: they rarely cover a team, an office and the travel LP and founder relationships need.

Technique · Kevin Moore, Hunter Somerville and Tom Duffy

Build operations before the portfolio outgrows them

Kevin says the most underrated part of running a firm is operations: "There's so much operational nuances to growing a firm, especially an investment platform that is not talked about." His current puzzle is how to add value consistently as his portfolio doubles. Hunter says the back office needs the same thought: what to outsource, what to bring in-house and when, and when to hire a senior operator, because compliance and operations are where mistakes and fraud happen. Tom Duffy of TIFF looks at whether a manager can institutionalize both how it invests and how it runs the business.

Technique · Andrea Hippeau, Lerer Hippeau

Give the growing portfolio its own owner

With fewer exits, companies stay in a portfolio longer, so it keeps growing while the investment team still has to find new deals. Andrea Hippeau, after 12 years at Lerer Hippeau, moved into a new role as head of portfolio management. She takes on promising companies that have been in the portfolio for two or three years, freeing the investment team to source, and uses AI to pull together the updates, board decks and financials the firm already collects.

Do this

  • Hire at principal level and train in your way of investing.
  • Write down what any new partner must share: strategy, culture, economics.
  • List what you'll outsource and what you'll bring in-house, and when.

What LPs will check

  • Who joined or left since the last fund, and why.
  • Whether new hires fit the strategy and culture.
  • Your fund administration, compliance and audit setup.

Stage 7 of 7 · Run the partnership

How should the partnership make decisions as it grows?

Short answerPick a decision process on purpose and keep it as the firm grows. Agree on how economics are shared before anyone needs to ask. Build the firm your strategy needs, not a copy of the largest firms.

Two things hold a partnership together over several funds: how it decides and how it shares. Both are easy to leave vague when the firm is small, and expensive to fix later.

Technique · David Hornik, Lobby Capital

Decide by conviction, not by vote

Lobby Capital has never voted on deals. A partner who's excited about a company brings it to the partnership, hears the concerns, does the work to answer them, and the partners meet the company. Then the partner can propose a term sheet.

It moves fast, often within a week, and the partner who'll sit on the board for years has done the work to believe in it. The risk, he acknowledges, is that some problems get missed.

Lesson · Neil Sequeira and Andrea Hippeau

Choose your structure on purpose

Neil spent 12 years at General Catalyst and watched its decision-making change as it grew: one partner meeting, then teams by sector, then an investment committee. At a large platform, he says, that structure is necessary. He started Defy as a small partnership that decides together in one room.

Lerer Hippeau doesn't vote either, Andrea says: a partner drives each deal, and a strong objection is taken very seriously. The firm's discussion now is about trusting gut more, because it has "talked ourselves out of doing deals that in retrospect we should have done."

Warning · Hunter Somerville, StepStone Group

Share the economics before you're asked

Many firms on their fifth to eighth funds have grown too big and developed unhealthy dynamics, Hunter says, and "a lot of them have not spread the economics around in an appropriate way." The result is a wave of partners spinning out to start smaller firms of their own. LPs then have to judge whether the spun-out team's record and dynamics still hold.

Technique · Hunter Somerville and Mike Collins

Build the firm your strategy needs

Mike Collins, founder and CEO of Alumni Ventures, describes taking "a generational approach to building our firm": a first decade focused on building the business with individual investors, and a second with new priorities: offices abroad and, eventually, other sources of capital. Planning the firm by decade makes it easier to say no to things that don't belong yet.

Culture

Brad says culture starts with leadership. Scout runs on the values he learned playing rugby at West Point, trust, love and commitment, and applies them to how the firm builds the fund, who it works with and how it spends its time.

Do this

  • Write down how the partnership makes decisions, and who can say no.
  • Agree carry and fee sharing, and how they change as people join or leave.
  • Write a one-page plan for what the firm is, and isn't, by Fund IV.

What LPs will check

  • How decisions are made, and who has really made them.
  • How economics are shared across the team.
  • Whether the firm's growth fits its strategy.

The Fund II checklist

Thirty-five steps drawn from this playbook, grouped by stage. Most belong in Fund I, years before the next raise. The PDF adds tick boxes and a quarterly tracker.

VC10X · Fund II and Beyond

Stage 1: During Fund I

  1. Write down what Fund I must prove for Fund II.
  2. Track that evidence every quarter.
  3. Keep a list of LPs who passed or were too big for Fund I, and update them.
  4. Estimate how much of Fund II your Fund I LPs can cover.
  5. Identify the LP types that can grow with you, and start meeting them.

Stage 2: Keep your promises

  1. Record each fund's promises: company count, ownership, check sizes, sectors.
  2. Write down what the fund will avoid.
  3. Report against those promises once a year.
  4. Explain any change of strategy to LPs before it happens.
  5. For any new area, write down why you'll win there and how you'll get access.

Stage 3: Between funds

  1. Fix the year's reporting dates, and meet every one.
  2. Avoid portfolio structures that delay K-1s.
  3. Add a tailored note for your largest LPs.
  4. Tell LPs about bad news yourself, before they hear it elsewhere.
  5. See LPs in person when you travel, and hold an annual meeting.

Stage 4: Size the next fund

  1. Set the company count for the next fund.
  2. Check round sizes and the ownership you can realistically get.
  3. Set the share of the fund held for follow-ons.
  4. Build the fund size from those three numbers.
  5. Decide how much demand above target you'll turn away.

Stage 5: Run the raise

  1. Talk to every existing LP about their re-up before you announce.
  2. Find out which LP groups are actively committing right now.
  3. Set a target close date and plan the process to fit it.
  4. Keep investing time protected during the raise.
  5. Close at target rather than holding the fund open.

Stage 6: Grow the team

  1. Hire at principal level and train people in your way of investing.
  2. Agree what any new partner must share before they join.
  3. Decide what to outsource and what to bring in-house, and when.
  4. Put someone in charge of supporting the older portfolio companies.
  5. Budget fees for people first.

Stage 7: Run the partnership

  1. Write down how the partnership decides, and who can say no.
  2. Agree how carry and fees are shared, and how that changes over time.
  3. Review the decision process each fund as the team grows.
  4. Write a one-page plan for what the firm is, and isn't, by Fund IV.
  5. Name the values the firm runs on, and use them to decide.

Frequently asked questions

Is a second venture fund easier to raise than the first?
Usually, if Fund I is going well. Marlon Nichols of MaC Venture Capital says institutional LPs often underwrite up to three funds when things go well, so you're not starting from zero. But only about 200 of more than 900 Fund Is raised in 2021 and 2022 had raised a successor by early 2026, according to a count Matt Curtolo cites.
What do LPs look at when deciding to re-up?
Performance first, then whether you did what you said. Marlon Nichols says Fund II is judged mainly on TVPI and the health of the portfolio companies, while DPI matters more by Fund III. TX Zhuo of Fika Ventures says being accountable to your stated ownership targets and sectors builds the trust that leads to re-ups.
How long does it take to raise Fund II?
It depends heavily on the market. Marlon Nichols says MaC's Fund I took 18 to 24 months, Fund II about six months and Fund III about nine. Jonathan Lacoste of Ambition Ventures closed his second fund in just under five months by running a deliberately tight process.
How big should a second venture fund be?
As big as the strategy needs and no bigger. Marlon Nichols works out fund size from the number of companies, target ownership and reserves. Hunter Somerville of StepStone Group says a seed fund that grows to $100 to $200 million needs to own around 10 percent of its companies.
What are the most common mistakes in Funds I and II?
Hiring, according to Hunter Somerville of StepStone Group: bringing in senior people from other firms who won't adapt, and adding partners who disrupt a small team's chemistry. He recommends hiring promising juniors and training them.
